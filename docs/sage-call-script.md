# The call script — Dr. Shashi Kad, SAGE Sustainability

**Monday 5 October 2026, 11:30–12:00 IST · Google Meet · 30 minutes**

Keep this open in a second window. Background is in `docs/sage-call-2026-10-05.md`; the strategy
is in `docs/sage-call-brief.md`. This file is what to actually say.

---

## 1. What you are pitching — one sentence

**You are not pitching the product. You are pitching a test.**

> *"I want to run one of your live BRSR engagements through this, free, so I can find out where
> it breaks."*

That is the whole pitch. Everything else in this document exists to earn the right to say it and
to make it easy for her to say yes.

**Why this and not the product:** she asked *"how will that tool be of use to us or anyone."*
The honest answer is *I believe I know, and I have not proved it.* Pretending otherwise to
someone who is certified in GRI, SBTi, CDP and Integrated Reporting, and who runs a
90%-referral practice, is the one move that loses her permanently. Conceding the gap before she
finds it is what earned the same-day reply in the first place. **Do that again, deliberately.**

**What you are NOT doing tomorrow:** not selling, not quoting a price, not asking for a job.
A job converts the thing you own into labour and turns you from a founder-peer into an
applicant. Don't.

---

## 2. How to pitch — the posture

**You are a builder bringing her a question, not a vendor bringing her a solution.**

Four rules:

1. **Concede first, always.** Every claim you make should arrive with its own limitation
   attached. "It's built and working — and nobody outside has run it on a real client yet."
2. **Let her correct you.** The call succeeds if she talks more than you. Your job in minutes
   11–23 is to shut up and write.
3. **Use her language, not yours.** Her practice runs on **"guided traverse → independent
   traverse."** Collect *is* a handover mechanism. Say it in her words and she will see the fit
   faster than any demo can show it.
4. **Never lead with the ask.** A pitch before a diagnosis is a worse pitch. Ask where SAGE
   loses hours first; her answer writes your ask for you.

**Energy:** calm, specific, curious. Not eager. She has no demand problem — 65+ clients, 90%
repeat or referral, ten years. Anyone who arrives needing something from her feels like a risk
to a reputation she has spent a decade building.

## 4. The thirty minutes, with the words

### 0:00–3:00 · Open by answering her question

Don't do a background intro. She booked the call; she knows who you are. Go straight at it.

> "Thanks for making the time, and thanks for the reply — I know the ask in my email was
> vague.
>
> You asked one question: how this would be of use to you or to anyone. I want to answer that
> honestly, and the honest answer has a weakness in it, so let me just lead with the weakness.
>
> About two hundred and forty consultants have used the free part of this over five months.
> Almost none of them have come back. And I think I know why: a BRSR gap analysis is a
> once-a-year job per client. Even someone who likes the tool has no reason to open it again
> next week.
>
> The part that actually recurs — chasing the numbers out of a client's team, the back and
> forth before a figure is defensible — that's the part I've built and that nobody outside has
> really run yet.
>
> So rather than describe it, can I show you the first pass, and then mostly be corrected?"

**Why this works:** it answers her exact question, it concedes before she probes, and it sets
her up as the expert rather than the prospect. It also makes your later ask obvious and small.

⚠️ **Say "about two hundred and forty", not "241".** A precise number invites "and how many
pay?" An approximate one keeps the conversation on the shape of the problem.

### 3:00–11:00 · The anonymised first pass

Share your screen. **You already generated it, so there is no loading and no typing.**

Walk it in exactly this order, and say roughly this:

**(a) The three statuses — 60 seconds**

> "This is a mid-size textile exporter, first-time filer, anonymised. The tool has gone through
> all one hundred and eight Section C disclosures and sorted them into three buckets: what they
> can pull from filings they already make, what's partly there and needs verification, and what
> they have to collect fresh.
>
> That sorting is the whole product, really. The value isn't the list — it's knowing which
> third of the list you don't have to chase."

**(b) One expanded row — 3 minutes. This is the most important minute of the call.**

Expand a **Principle 6** row (energy or GHG).

> "Here's what's under one row. The SEBI wording, verbatim. The ICAI page it's on. Where the
> data usually lives inside the company. What a complete answer actually contains. And the
> emission factor, with its version and the financial year it applies to.
>
> That last one is the reason the product exists. A Scope 2 number computed on last year's grid
> factor still adds up — it's just computed on a basis nobody can cite now. So the version
> travels with the number everywhere, including on the public factor page."

**Pause here.** If she is going to engage with anything, it is this. She is CDP- and
GRI-certified; citation discipline is her native language.

**(c) Where it deliberately stops — 90 seconds. Say this out loud; don't skip it.**

> "Three things it deliberately doesn't do.
>
> It doesn't decide materiality. It suggests a starting shortlist by industry, and it says on
> the screen that a compliant assessment needs a stakeholder process it can't run for you.
>
> It doesn't claim anything is assured. It's built so a figure can be *defended* — source,
> owner, evidence, factor version — which is a different thing.
>
> And it doesn't make the wider sustainability story look finished. That was the point of my
> first email. A first pass that looks complete is worse than one that looks unfinished,
> because the client stops."

**(d) Collect, in 90 seconds, framed in her language**

> "The second half is the bit I'd want your view on. You assign the fields to the people inside
> the client who actually hold them, they get their own link with no login, they fill in their
> own numbers, it chases them on a cadence, and every figure comes back with who it came from
> and whether it was typed by that person or read out of a document.
>
> The reason I think it might fit SAGE specifically: it's a handover mechanism. Your
> guided-to-independent traverse — that's the shape I've been building without having a name
> for it."

Then stop. Hand the call over:

> "That's the tour. Where does the first pass get the balance wrong?"

**Do not show:** Collect with real campaigns in it, the proposal/fee builder, or any screen you
have not personally clicked today.

### 11:00–23:00 · Her critique and your four questions

**This is the valuable part of the call. Write visibly. Do not defend anything.**

When she criticises something, the only correct responses are:

- "Say more about that."
- "Where have you seen that go wrong?"
- "So if I did X instead, does that fix it, or does it just move it?"

**Never** "yes, but" and **never** "that's actually already handled." If she misread a screen,
the screen is wrong, not her.

Your four questions, in priority order. Ask the first two regardless.

**Q1 — the one you promised in the email:**
> "Where does the first pass get the balance wrong?"

**Q2 — the one that writes the roadmap:**
> "Where does SAGE collect the same number twice? Across BRSR, CDP, EcoVadis — where is a
> client giving you the same figure in two different shapes?"

**Q3 — the biggest long-term prize:**
> "You lead the curriculum at Green Skills Academy. I've built one reference page for each of
> the one hundred and eight disclosures, and an eight-module teaching pack from them. I'd like
> your view on whether that's any use as learning material — not as a product, just whether
> it's the right shape for people who are learning this."

**Q4 — the one that separates polite interest from real intent:**
> "What would SAGE have to stop doing to use something like this?"

**And the follow-up that gets you the real requirements** — ask this when she mentions any
specific difficulty:

> "Can you walk me through the last metric that was genuinely difficult? Where was the source,
> what was wrong with it, how many rounds did it take, and who signed off at the end?"

That answer is worth more than everything else in the call. Write it down verbatim.

### 23:00–28:00 · Where it's going

Only if there is time, and only framed as in-progress.

> "Where I'm taking it: a client collects each number once, and it carries across BRSR, CDP and
> EcoVadis without being re-entered. Principle 6 is mapped at field level today — energy,
> water, GHG, waste — because those are what clients get asked for over and over. The rest is
> still principle-level, so I'd be overselling it if I said it was done."

⚠️ **If she asks about GRESB:** *"Not built. It's a real-estate and infrastructure benchmark and
it would need its own vocabulary — I didn't want to fake a mapping."* **Do not claim GRESB.**

### 28:00–30:00 · The ask

**Pick based on what she actually responded to.** Do not read out the whole ladder.

**If she described manual, repeated, spreadsheet work →** go for the engagement:

> "Here's what I'd like, and it's small. Is there one client where SAGE is chasing BRSR data
> right now? I'll set the whole collection up myself, free — you keep the relationship and the
> output. I get to watch where it breaks. That's the only thing I actually want out of this."

**If she lit up on training and curriculum →** go for the Academy:

> "Could we try one co-branded module? I'd build it, your name on it, no cost either way. If
> it's useful to Green Skills Academy, we do more. If it isn't, you've lost an hour."

**If she talked about one specific client's data problem →** go narrow:

> "Could I set up that one collection, for that one client, and show you what comes back?"

**If she was mainly curious and critical, with no specific pain →** go smallest:

> "Could I send you the next version when the thing you flagged is fixed, and get ten more
> minutes of this?"

**Then close, whatever happened:**

> "Either way — thank you. This was the critique I asked for and I'll act on it. I'll send you
> one short note today with just the thing we agreed, nothing else."

## 5. If she asks — answers, close to verbatim

**"How is one client's data separated from another's?"**
> "Firm-level separation on collections is in — each firm owns its campaigns and every
> campaign-level query filters by it. The row-level hardening on individual field writes is the
> next commit. I'd rather tell you precisely where the line is than say 'it's secure'."

**"Do you have per-user accounts for a team?"**
> "No. One passcode per firm today. Real per-person accounts with attribution are the next
> build after row-level hardening."
⚠️ **Never say "seats."** They do not exist. SAGE's 17 people would share one passcode.

**"Does it map to CDP and EcoVadis?"**
> "At field level for Principle 6 — the environment figures clients get asked for repeatedly.
> Every other principle is principle-level only. And nothing's invented: the vocabulary comes
> from the published CDP questionnaire areas and EcoVadis' documented criteria, not from me."

**"Is the data assured?"**
> "No, and the product never claims it is. It's built so a figure can be defended — source,
> owner, evidence, factor version. That's a different thing from assured."

**"Who else is using it?"**
> "More than two hundred and twenty users, mostly independent consultants. No firm is a
> customer. SAGE would be the first — which is exactly why a pilot is worth more to me than a
> licence fee would be."

**"Did you build this alone?"**
> "I built it with a practising ESG consultant, Priya Ranjan. She shaped what it collects and
> in what order."

**"What does it cost?"**
> "There's no public pricing and I haven't validated any. Priced per engagement, onboarded
> manually. I'd rather set a price after someone has actually run a cycle on it than guess
> now."
⚠️ **Do not name a number. Not even a range. Not even "around".**

**"Do you want to work for us?" / "Are you looking for a job?"**
> "I'm not looking for a role — I'd rather build. If SAGE ever wanted something built to your
> spec, that's a conversation I'd love to have. But today I just want one engagement to test
> against."

**"We have our own tools / we're happy with spreadsheets."**
> "That's useful to know, and honestly spreadsheets are what most of my users compare against
> too. The thing I'd ask is narrower: not whether you'd replace anything, but whether the
> chasing part costs you hours you'd rather not spend."

**"How did you find my piece?"** — answer simply and truthfully. Don't mention the other firms
you emailed.

## 7. If it goes sideways

**She joins late or cuts it to 15 minutes.** Drop the tour to 3 minutes — statuses, one
expanded row, where it stops. Then ask Q1 and Q2 only, then the ask. Protect the ask.

**She brings a colleague.** Good sign. Re-ask the opening question to the room, and direct the
"where does it get the balance wrong" question at whoever does the hands-on work.

**She is lukewarm and non-specific.** Don't push up the ladder. Take the smallest ask — send
the next version, ask for ten more minutes. A second call you earned beats a pilot she
half-agreed to.

**She finds a real error on screen.** Say so immediately and plainly: *"That's wrong. Thank
you — I'll fix it today and tell you when it's out."* Then actually do it, and put it in the
note. A found-and-fixed error inside 24 hours is a better demonstration than a clean demo.

**She asks something you don't know.** *"I don't know. Let me check and come back to you in the
note."* Never guess at a regulatory fact in front of someone certified in four frameworks.

**It drifts into general ESG conversation.** Let it, for a while — she is interesting and
rapport is the point. But protect the last three minutes for the ask.

## 9. The win condition

**She gives a real critique, and says yes to one small next step.** That is the whole bar.

A critique with warmth and no commitment is still a decent outcome. An Academy conversation is
a bonus on a different timeline. Pilot, partnership and anything larger come from calls two,
three and four — and only if this one earns them.

**The single most likely way to lose this call is to oversell.** If you catch yourself claiming
something is proven, stop mid-sentence and correct it. She will respect the correction more
than she would have believed the claim.
